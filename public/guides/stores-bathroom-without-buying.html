Which Stores Let You Use the Bathroom Without Buying Anything

A chain-by-chain look at who will let you in, who will probably let you in if you ask, and who has stopped saying yes. Policies checked against current company statements and reporting as of September 2026.

The short answer

If you need a bathroom right now and do not want to buy anything, your best odds are, in roughly this order: a public library or government building, a big-box store (Walmart, Target, Home Depot, Lowe's), a mall, a hotel lobby, and then a fast-food restaurant or grocery store. Coffee shops are no longer the easy default. Starbucks changed its rules in January 2025 and now officially reserves restrooms for paying customers.

One caveat runs through everything below. Almost no chain publishes a binding, nationwide rule about non-customers, and a store that is friendly in a suburb can be locked down in a dense downtown. Treat everything here as a likelihood, not a promise.

Chain-by-chain rundown

Very likely to say yes: public libraries and government buildings

Public libraries are the most reliable free restroom in most American towns. They are open to everyone during posted hours, no purchase or library card is needed, and the restrooms tend to be maintained. City halls, courthouses, transit stations, and visitor centers are similar. The limitation is hours: many branches close early on weekends or are shut on Sundays.

Likely to say yes: Walmart, Target, Home Depot, Lowe's

Walmart has no published policy restricting restrooms to paying customers; its restrooms near the front entrance are open to anyone who walks in during store hours, and nobody checks receipts. Target is similar. Its stores have public restrooms, and while store employees are allowed to ask non-shoppers to be quick, there is no purchase requirement. Home Depot and Lowe's both have public restrooms in every store, generally near the front or the customer service desk, and neither company has published a purchase requirement. A few shoppers have reported individual Home Depot locations asking for a receipt, so this is not universal, but the typical experience is an open door. The common thread is size: big-box restrooms are built for high traffic, and one more person is not a burden.

Usually yes: malls and hotel lobbies

Enclosed shopping malls almost always have shared public restrooms near the food court or along a service corridor, and nobody expects you to buy anything. Outdoor "lifestyle center" malls are less consistent; restrooms there may be inside individual stores.

Hotel lobbies are an underused option. Most hotels have a restroom off the lobby, and staff rarely stop someone who walks in purposefully. No formal policy grants access, so it depends on the hotel. Higher-end city properties are more likely to have a doorman who asks what you need; mid-range hotels usually do not.

It depends on the location: McDonald's and other fast food

McDonald's has no published corporate policy on non-customer restroom use. Most restaurants are franchisee-owned, and the franchisee decides. In suburban and highway locations the restroom is typically unlocked. In dense urban areas and 24-hour locations it is common to find a coded lock with the code printed on your receipt. If you see a keypad, assume you will need to buy something or ask.

The same pattern holds for Burger King, Wendy's, Dunkin', Taco Bell, Subway, and Chipotle. None publish a "customers only" rule, but individual operators frequently enforce one.

It depends: grocery stores

Supermarkets are a mixed bag. Many have a restroom, but it is often at the back of the store or behind a door near customer service, and in some regions it is locked by default. Trader Joe's, Whole Foods, Sprouts, Safeway, Ralphs, and Vons have all been reported to lock restrooms at some locations, particularly in Southern California. None of the major grocery chains publish a purchase requirement, so you are rarely told no outright; you may just have to find an employee with a key. In California, food stores over roughly 20,000 square feet or with in-store dining are required by health code to have a customer restroom, which is why the big stores nearly always do.

It depends, trending toward no: Barnes & Noble

Barnes & Noble does not publish a restroom policy. Historically its stores were a dependable place to use a restroom while browsing. In the last few years, several high-traffic locations, including stores in Manhattan and Washington, D.C., have installed keypad locks with the code printed on receipts, effectively making the restroom purchase-only. Suburban stores are more likely to still have an open door.

Probably no without a purchase: Starbucks

This is the one people ask about most, and the answer has changed. In May 2018, after two Black men were arrested at a Philadelphia store while waiting for a meeting without ordering, Starbucks announced that anyone could sit in its cafes and use its restrooms without buying anything. That open-door policy lasted about six and a half years.

In January 2025, under new CEO Brian Niccol, Starbucks reversed it. The company posted a "Coffeehouse Code of Conduct" in every North American store stating that cafes, patios, and restrooms are for customers who make a purchase. The code also bans harassment, panhandling, smoking, and outside alcohol, and says people who violate it will be asked to leave. As of September 2026 that code remains in effect; we found no announcement reversing or softening it.

There is some room in the official language. Starbucks said it recognized there would be times a customer needs the restroom before ordering, and that stores would comply with any local law requiring access for non-customers. In practice a barista can still say yes if you ask, and many do. But the official policy is now purchase-first, and you should not be surprised to be told no or pointed to a coded lock.

Usually no without a purchase: gas stations

Gas stations are the most likely of all these categories to enforce "customers only," and the most likely to lock the door and hand you a key attached to something large, especially small independent and urban stations. The exceptions are large travel centers such as Buc-ee's, Love's, Pilot, and Wawa, where restrooms are a selling point and open to anyone who walks in.

How Restroom Access Acts (Ally's Law) fit in

Everything above describes customer policy, meaning what a business chooses to allow. Separately, more than 20 states have passed a Restroom Access Act, often called Ally's Law after Ally Bain, an Illinois teenager with Crohn's disease who was denied an employee restroom in 2004. These laws require a retail business that has an employee-only restroom to let a customer use it if the customer has an eligible medical condition (such as Crohn's disease, ulcerative colitis, an ostomy, or, in some states, pregnancy) and presents evidence such as a doctor's note or an official access card.

These laws cover people with a qualifying condition, not the general public, and they override a "customers only" sign; Starbucks explicitly said its 2025 code would yield to laws of this kind. If you have a qualifying condition, our guide to restroom access laws lists which states have one and what you need to show.

How to ask

A short, direct question works better than trying to slip past. A few things that help:

  • Ask the person at the register or counter, not a manager and not someone stocking shelves. The cashier is the person who can say yes fastest.
  • Keep it to one sentence. "Hi, could I use your restroom?" is enough.
  • If the store has a coded lock and you can afford it, buying the cheapest item on the counter is the most reliable unlock; a pack of gum or a banana is often under two dollars. This is a practical option, not an obligation. Nobody should have to spend money to use a toilet.
  • If the answer is no, do not argue. Walk to the next option on the list.

Why libraries and public buildings are the safest bet

Libraries land at the top of this list for a structural reason. A library restroom is paid for by the public and open by design, not by the goodwill of a franchisee or store manager. It does not depend on how busy the shift is or whether a new CEO changed a policy last winter. Parks, transit stations, and city buildings work the same way, and none of them will turn you away.

FAQ

Does Starbucks still let you use the bathroom without buying anything?

Officially, no. Since January 2025, the Starbucks Coffeehouse Code of Conduct states that restrooms are for paying customers, ending the open-door policy that ran from 2018. Starbucks has said it will follow local laws requiring access for non-customers, and an individual barista may still say yes, but you should expect to be asked to buy something, especially at busy urban stores with coded locks.

Is it legal for a store to refuse to let me use the bathroom?

In most cases, yes. U.S. businesses are generally required to provide restrooms only to employees, and they can restrict customer restrooms to paying customers. The exceptions are state Restroom Access Acts (for people with qualifying medical conditions) and local health codes that require restrooms in larger food stores or sit-down restaurants.

Which stores have public restrooms that anyone can use?

Walmart, Target, Home Depot, Lowe's, and enclosed shopping malls are the most consistent. None publish a purchase requirement and receipts are not checked. Public libraries are even more reliable, though their hours are shorter.

Can I use the bathroom at McDonald's if I don't buy anything?

Often, but not always. McDonald's has no corporate rule; each franchisee sets its own practice. Suburban and highway locations usually leave the restroom unlocked, while city and tourist-area locations often use a coded lock tied to a receipt.

What do I do if I have Crohn's or another medical condition and a store says no?

If you are in a state with a Restroom Access Act, tell the employee you have a medical condition covered by the law and show your documentation or access card. The store is required to let you use its employee restroom, even if it has a customers-only sign. In a state without such a law, head for the nearest library, big-box store, or mall.
