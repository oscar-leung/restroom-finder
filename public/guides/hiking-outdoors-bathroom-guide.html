The Outdoor Bathroom Guide: Trailheads, Vault Toilets, and Leave No Trace

Everything you need to know about bathrooms on hiking trails — from what to expect at the trailhead to what to do when there's no toilet for twenty miles.

Nobody plans a hike around bathroom access, but everybody has thought about it at mile four. The good news: once you know what facilities exist where, how to survive a vault toilet, and what to do when there's no toilet at all, the whole subject stops being a source of anxiety and becomes just another part of trip planning, like water and layers.

What to Expect, and Where

Outdoor restroom quality follows a predictable gradient: the farther you get from a parking lot, the fewer amenities you'll find.

Visitor centers and developed campgrounds are your best bet. National park visitor centers, state park offices, and larger campgrounds usually have real plumbing — flush toilets, running water, soap, sometimes even heat. If your route passes one, use it, whether or not you think you need to.

Trailheads are a coin flip. Popular trailheads in national parks and forests often have a vault or pit toilet — a sturdy outhouse over a sealed underground tank. Less-trafficked trailheads may have nothing but a parking area and a signboard. Land-agency websites (NPS, US Forest Service, BLM) usually list trailhead amenities, and it's worth checking before you drive an hour into the mountains assuming there's a toilet waiting.

The backcountry has, with rare exceptions, nothing. A few heavily used wilderness areas maintain privies at established camps, but assume that once you leave the trailhead, you are the bathroom infrastructure. That's what the Leave No Trace section below is for.

Vault Toilets: What They Are and How to Survive Them

A vault toilet is a non-flush toilet that sits over a large sealed underground vault, pumped out periodically by the managing agency. No water, no plumbing, usually no electricity. They're a well-engineered solution — properly designed ones vent odors up a stack above the roofline — but the experience varies with maintenance and weather.

A few things worth knowing:

  • Bring your own toilet paper. Agencies stock TP when they can, but busy weekends outrun the supply constantly. A partial roll in a zip-top bag lives permanently in a well-prepared hiker's pack.
  • Bring hand sanitizer. There is no sink. Ever. A small bottle clipped to your pack strap solves this forever.
  • Close the lid when you're done. This isn't just courtesy — the venting system works best when the lid is down, because it forces airflow up the vent stack instead of out the toilet opening. An open lid makes the whole building smell worse for everyone after you.
  • Expect worse smells in heat. Warm weather accelerates decomposition in the vault, so a July afternoon vault toilet in the desert is a different experience than the same toilet on a cool October morning. Breathe through your mouth, be quick, and remember it beats the alternative.
  • Don't throw trash in the vault. Bottles, diapers, and wrappers make the pump-out process miserable and expensive. TP only.

Plan Ahead: The Cheapest Bathroom Strategy

Leave No Trace's first principle is "plan ahead and prepare," and it applies to bathrooms as much as anything else.

Go before you go. Use the visitor center or trailhead toilet before you start hiking, even if it's only a "maybe." A pit stop at the trailhead is free; a cathole at mile two because you skipped it is not.

Know your route's facilities. Check the land agency's page for your trail. Note where toilets exist — trailhead, junction privies, backcountry camps — and roughly when you'll pass them. On a day hike near town, it also helps to find real restrooms near the trailhead with Gotta Go so you know your last flush-toilet opportunity before the pavement ends.

Time your fluids sensibly. Don't dehydrate yourself to avoid peeing outdoors — dehydration on a trail is far more dangerous than any bathroom logistics. But chugging a liter in the car and none after is a recipe for an urgent stop twenty minutes in. Sip steadily instead of front-loading.

Know your own patterns. Coffee, long drives, and early starts do predictable things to most digestive systems. Build your morning around that reality.

When There's No Toilet: The Leave No Trace Rules

Human waste in the backcountry spreads pathogens like giardia, pollutes water sources, and ruins campsites. The Leave No Trace guidance boils down to a few rules.

The cathole method

For solid waste, the standard practice in most forested and soil-rich environments is a cathole:

  • Get 200 feet away — about 70 adult paces — from any water source, trail, or campsite. This is the single most important rule, because it keeps pathogens out of the water everyone else is filtering.
  • Dig 6 to 8 inches deep and about 4 to 6 inches wide. A lightweight trowel makes this a one-minute job; a stick or boot heel makes it a frustrating five-minute job. The 6–8 inch depth matters: it's where soil microbes are most active, so waste breaks down fastest, and it's deep enough that animals won't dig it up.
  • Choose good soil if you can — dark, organic, with sun exposure. Decomposition is faster there.
  • Cover and disguise the hole when finished with the original dirt, then top it with leaves or duff. Some hikers plant a small stick upright to warn future diggers.

If you're camped somewhere for multiple days, spread catholes out rather than concentrating them in one area.

Pack out your toilet paper

Toilet paper decomposes far slower than waste, animals dig it up, and "TP flowers" behind boulders are the most common Leave No Trace failure on popular trails. The modern standard is to pack it out. The easiest technique is the dog-bag trick: bring a few opaque dog waste bags, put used TP in one, knot it, and drop it into a dedicated zip-top bag in an outside pack pocket. It's odorless, weighs nothing, and goes in the first trash can you see. Never burn TP in the backcountry — it's caused real wildfires.

Wag bags: when you pack out everything

In some environments, catholes don't work: high-alpine zones with no soil, deserts where nothing decomposes, and slot canyons where you can't get 200 feet from water. In these places land managers require you to pack out all solid waste using a wag bag — a commercial waste kit with gelling powder, a sealable outer bag, and TP included. Mt. Whitney and the Zion Narrows are the classic examples where wag bags are mandatory and often handed out with your permit; many other alpine, desert, and river corridors require them too. Check regulations when you get your permit. They're less gross than they sound — the gelling agent neutralizes odor — and they're the reason those fragile places are still pleasant to visit.

Urine is the easy one

Pee is much lower-impact than solid waste — it dilutes quickly. Just go well away from water sources, trails, and camp, and aim for rocks or bare ground rather than plants (salt-craving animals will dig up vegetation that's been peed on). No hole, no packing out — though in narrow canyons like the Zion Narrows, the rule is to pee directly into the river, where dilution handles it better than the sandbars can.

Gear Notes for Women

Two pieces of gear have quietly become standard in a lot of packs. A pee funnel (brands like pStyle, Freshette, or GoGirl) lets you pee standing up without undressing — handy in cold weather, on exposed terrain, or anywhere privacy is scarce. They take a little at-home practice but many users swear by them. A pee cloth (the Kula Cloth is the best-known) is a snap-on antimicrobial fabric square that replaces TP for urination entirely — it hangs on your pack, dries fast, and means less paper to pack out. Neither is required; both solve real problems.

Winter Considerations

Frozen ground makes catholes impossible, and snow is not a disposal system — everything buried in snow reappears, perfectly preserved, at the melt. In snow-covered terrain the correct move is almost always to pack out solid waste in a wag bag. Note also that some trailhead vault toilets stay open year-round while others are locked seasonally; check ahead. And figure out your layer logistics before you're fumbling with bibs in a windstorm.

Teaching Kids

Kids do best when outdoor bathroom breaks are treated as normal and practiced early. Frame the cathole as part of the adventure — let them help dig and count out the 70 paces. Carry extra TP and sanitizer, expect zero advance warning, and make the trailhead-toilet stop a non-negotiable ritual. A kid who learns Leave No Trace at six will do it automatically at sixteen.

The Backcountry Bathroom Kit

The whole kit weighs a few ounces and fits in a quart zip-top bag:

  • Lightweight trowel (aluminum or plastic)
  • Toilet paper in its own zip-top bag
  • Opaque dog bags plus a dedicated pack-out zip-top bag
  • Hand sanitizer
  • Wag bags, where required or in winter
  • Optional: pee funnel and/or pee cloth

FAQ

Do hiking trails have bathrooms?

Some do at the trailhead — usually a vault or pit toilet at popular trailheads in national parks and forests — but most trails have no facilities beyond the parking area. Check the managing agency's website for your specific trailhead, and assume the backcountry has nothing.

What is a vault toilet?

A vault toilet is a non-flush outhouse built over a large sealed underground tank that's periodically pumped out. There's no running water, so bring your own toilet paper and hand sanitizer, and close the lid when you're done to keep the venting system working properly.

How deep should a cathole be?

Six to eight inches deep and four to six inches wide, dug at least 200 feet (about 70 paces) from any water source, trail, or campsite. That depth puts waste in the biologically active soil layer where it decomposes fastest, and keeps animals from digging it up.

Can I bury toilet paper in a cathole?

The modern Leave No Trace standard is to pack it out instead — TP decomposes slowly and animals frequently excavate it. Use the dog-bag trick: seal used TP in an opaque dog waste bag inside a dedicated zip-top bag, and toss it in the first trash can you pass. Never burn it.

Where are wag bags required?

In fragile environments where waste can't decompose or be safely buried — Mt. Whitney and the Zion Narrows are the best-known examples, along with many high-alpine, desert, and river-canyon areas. Requirements are spelled out with your permit, and some trailheads hand them out free.
