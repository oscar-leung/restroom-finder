The Best Restroom Finder Apps and Maps, Compared

An honest look at Flush, Refuge Restrooms, Google Maps, international options, city-run programs, and our own app — including where each one falls short.

Full disclosure before we go any further: this guide is written and published by the team behind Gotta Go, one of the apps compared below. We have an obvious interest in you trying our app, so weigh our take on it accordingly. We've done our best to describe every option fairly — including the real advantages competitors have over us and the honest limitations of our own app — because a comparison you can't trust isn't worth reading. Where we're unsure of a detail about another app, we say so rather than guessing.

Needing a restroom and not knowing where the nearest one is ranks among the most universally stressful small emergencies. It's worse if you're traveling, managing a condition like IBS or Crohn's, out with small children, or using a wheelchair. The good news: there are several genuinely useful tools for finding a public bathroom, and most are free. The catch is that they differ a lot in coverage, philosophy, and speed. Here's how the major restroom finder apps and maps actually compare.

Flush Toilet Finder

If you've searched for a bathroom finder app before, you've probably run into Flush. It's one of the best-known apps in this category, and for good reason: it has built up a large crowdsourced database of public toilets over many years, spanning a huge number of locations worldwide. The interface is refreshingly simple — open the app, see toilets near you on a map, tap one for directions.

Flush is ratings-focused: listings can be marked with details like whether a fee is required or whether the toilet is accessible, and users rate locations so you get a rough sense of quality before you walk over. It also works offline in a basic way, handy when traveling without data.

The main trade-off is that the database is only as fresh as its submissions. Historically, users generally couldn't edit listings added by other people, which means an outdated entry — a cafe that closed, a restroom that's now customers-only — tends to linger until someone flags it. That's not a knock unique to Flush; it's the central challenge of every crowdsourced map. If raw database size is what you care about most, Flush is still very hard to beat.

Refuge Restrooms

Refuge Restrooms is a different kind of project, and an important one. It's an open-source, volunteer-driven database focused on safe restroom access for transgender, intersex, and gender-nonconforming people. Listings emphasize whether a restroom is gender-neutral or single-stall and whether it's accessible — details that matter enormously to people for whom a public restroom can be a site of harassment rather than mere inconvenience.

Refuge grew out of an earlier project called Safe2Pee and has been maintained by the community for years. Beyond the app and website themselves, Refuge publishes an open API, which means other developers can build on its data instead of starting from zero. We should disclose here, warmly and gratefully: Gotta Go uses Refuge's open data as one of its sources. Projects like Refuge are the reason the whole ecosystem of restroom finders is as good as it is, and if safe access is your primary concern, we'd point you to Refuge directly without hesitation.

Its limitations are the honest ones of any volunteer project: coverage is uneven and the interface is more utilitarian than slick. None of that diminishes what it does well.

Google Maps

Google Maps is the tool everyone tries first, and sometimes it's all you need. Searching "restroom" or "public toilet" will surface parks, transit stations, and some public facilities, and because nearly every business is on Google Maps, you can often find a fast-food place or department store with a usable bathroom in seconds. Reviews sometimes mention restroom conditions, and the sheer ubiquity of the app means zero setup.

The weakness is that Google Maps was never designed as a restroom finder. Search quality for "restroom" varies wildly by city — sometimes you get a helpful list, sometimes a scattering of plumbing supply stores. More importantly, there are no restroom-specific filters: you can't ask it to show only free restrooms, only accessible ones, only gender-neutral ones, or only what's open right now as a restroom rather than as a business. A restaurant that's open isn't necessarily a restaurant that will let you use the bathroom. Google Maps is a great fallback and a poor specialist.

Where is Public Toilet and Other International Apps

If you're outside the United States, your best option may be an app most Americans have never heard of. Where is Public Toilet is a long-running app with particularly strong coverage in parts of Asia and elsewhere internationally, and many countries have their own local favorites — national toilet maps, government open-data apps, and regional crowdsourced projects. Australia, for example, has a well-regarded national public toilet map backed by government data.

The pattern to remember: US-built apps tend to be US-strongest, and vice versa. If you're traveling abroad, it's worth spending two minutes finding the locally dominant toilet map rather than assuming your usual app will cover you. These international options are often excellent within their home regions and thin outside them.

City-Run Maps and Programs (The Pit Stop and Friends)

Some of the most reliable restrooms anywhere come from city programs. San Francisco's Pit Stop program places staffed public toilets around the city, and the staffing is the key detail — an attended restroom stays clean and safe in a way an unattended one often doesn't. New York City publishes maps of public restrooms in parks and other facilities, and many other cities maintain similar open datasets or official maps.

When a city-run map exists for where you are, use it — the data comes straight from the operator, so it's usually accurate about locations and hours. The obvious limitation is right there in the name: these tools are city-limited — a great local layer, not a general solution, and you have to know the program exists to go looking for it.

Gotta Go (That's Us)

Gotta Go is our attempt to fix the specific failure modes we kept hitting in the tools above. It's a free web app built around one idea: when you need a restroom, you don't want to browse a map — you want an answer. So the core flow is one button. Tap it, and Gotta Go finds the closest restroom to you and points you there.

Beyond that, the design choices that set it apart:

  • Anyone can edit any listing. Unlike most crowdsourced maps, you're not locked out of fixing someone else's outdated entry. If a restroom closed or its hours changed, you can correct it on the spot — or remotely, from your couch, for places you know well.
  • Filters that match real needs: accessible, gender-neutral, free, and open-now.
  • No signup, no app store. It's a progressive web app — it opens in your browser instantly and can be added to your home screen. No account, no email, no download.

And the honest limitations, because we promised: Gotta Go is newer than Flush, and our contributor community is far smaller, so our database is smaller too. Coverage is strongest in US metro areas and thinner elsewhere. Reviews are currently stored on your own device rather than shared with other users — useful as a personal memory, not yet a community signal. We think the editing model and one-tap flow are worth those trade-offs, but you should know them going in. If that sounds useful, you can try Gotta Go free in your browser right now.

Which Restroom Finder Should You Use?

Honestly, the best answer for most people is more than one — they're all free, and coverage gaps differ.

  • Fastest one-tap answer: Gotta Go. The closest-restroom button plus no-signup web access makes it the quickest path from "uh oh" to walking directions.
  • Biggest raw database: Flush. Years of crowdsourced submissions add up, especially internationally.
  • Safety-focused access: Refuge Restrooms. If gender-neutral and safe restrooms are your priority, go to the community that built the map for exactly that.
  • Traveling internationally: Where is Public Toilet or the dominant local app for your destination — plus any official government toilet map.
  • Zero-setup fallback: Google Maps. It's already on your phone, and a well-chosen business search often works fine.
  • In SF, NYC, and similar cities: check the official city program or map too — operator data beats crowdsourced data when it exists.

FAQ

Are restroom finder apps free?

All of the options in this guide are free to use. Some app-store apps offer optional paid upgrades or show ads, but the core find-a-toilet function is free everywhere here. Gotta Go is entirely free with no account required.

What's the best alternative to the Flush app?

It depends on what you're missing. If Flush's stale listings are the problem, Gotta Go lets anyone edit any listing, which helps outdated entries get fixed faster. If you want safety-focused data, Refuge Restrooms is the strongest option. If you just want ubiquity with no new app, Google Maps is the practical fallback.

How accurate are crowdsourced restroom maps?

Generally good in dense, well-traveled areas and less reliable in rural areas or for older entries. Restrooms close, change hours, or become customers-only, and any crowdsourced database lags reality by some amount. Apps that make corrections easy — and city-run maps fed by official data — tend to stay freshest.

Can I find wheelchair-accessible or gender-neutral restrooms with these apps?

Yes, but support varies. Refuge Restrooms is built around gender-neutral and accessible listings. Gotta Go has dedicated accessible and gender-neutral filters. Flush marks accessibility on many listings. Google Maps notes wheelchair accessibility for businesses but can't filter restrooms specifically.

Do I need to download an app to use Gotta Go?

No. Gotta Go is a progressive web app, so it runs in any modern browser on your phone or computer with nothing to install and no signup. You can add it to your home screen so it opens like a native app.
