Where to Find Public Restrooms in New York City

Eight and a half million people, tens of millions of tourists, and famously almost nowhere to pee. Here's the real map of NYC's public bathrooms — libraries, parks, train halls, department stores, and the unwritten rules that make the whole system work.

New York has a lot of everything, except public toilets. By most counts the city offers one of the lowest ratios of public restrooms per capita of any major American city — a few toilets per 10,000 residents, which puts it behind not just Tokyo and Paris but behind plenty of mid-size U.S. cities too. The upshot: finding a bathroom in NYC is a learned skill, like hailing a cab in the rain or walking through Times Square without stopping. This guide covers where to actually go, borough by borough, and how to do it with a straight face. When you're in a hurry, check the nearest open restroom with Gotta Go and skip the guesswork.

The Reliable Classics: Libraries and Parks

The New York Public Library system is the city's unsung restroom network. NYPL branches — and there are dozens across Manhattan, the Bronx, and Staten Island, with Brooklyn and Queens running their own systems — have public restrooms, are genuinely open to everyone, and nobody will look at you twice. The Stavros Niarchos Foundation Library across from the main branch on Fifth Avenue is modern, multi-floor, and clean. You don't need a library card. You don't need to buy anything. This is as close to a guaranteed bathroom as New York gets, at least during library hours.

Then there's Bryant Park, whose public restroom is legitimately famous — it has attendants, fresh flowers, classical music, and has been called the nicest public toilet in America without much exaggeration. It sits just behind the main NYPL building at 42nd Street, and yes, there is sometimes a line, because New Yorkers know a good thing.

The Parks Department runs hundreds of "comfort stations" citywide. Quality varies from "fine" to "you've been warned," but they exist and they're free. Central Park has several — near the Delacorte Theater, by Bethesda Terrace, near Heckscher Playground at the south end, and at the Conservatory Garden uptown. Washington Square Park has a comfort station on its west side. Most park restrooms are open roughly from morning until dusk; they are not a late-night option.

Transit Hubs: Better Than You'd Think

Skip the subway — only a small handful of stations have open restrooms, and you don't want most of them anyway. The big rail terminals are a different story.

Grand Central Terminal has public restrooms on the lower-level dining concourse. They're busy, they're maintained, and the terminal is open long hours, making this one of Midtown East's most dependable stops.

Moynihan Train Hall — the airy Amtrak/LIRR hall across Eighth Avenue from Penn Station — has clean, modern restrooms, and the building itself is a pleasant place to be, which is not something anyone ever said about old Penn Station. Penn Station proper also has restrooms in the LIRR and NJ Transit concourses if you're already down there.

Port Authority Bus Terminal has restrooms on multiple levels. They are exactly as glamorous as you'd expect from a bus terminal, but they're open long hours and they function. In a pinch near Times Square, this beats wandering.

The Fulton Center transit hub and the Oculus at the World Trade Center both serve downtown, with the Oculus doubling as a mall — restrooms included.

Department Stores, Bookstores, and Food Halls

Retail is the great restroom workaround. Macy's Herald Square has restrooms on several floors of its enormous flagship; nobody is checking whether you intend to buy socks. Nordstrom's Manhattan flagship near Columbus Circle is newer and correspondingly nicer. Bloomingdale's on the Upper East Side works the same way. Department store restrooms tend to be tucked toward the back or on upper floors — follow the signs, take the escalator, act like you shop there.

Barnes & Noble locations reliably have customer restrooms, usually upstairs past the journals and board games. Ditto most Whole Foods stores, several of which (Columbus Circle, Bryant Park, Union Square) have seating areas where a restroom visit raises zero eyebrows.

Food halls are the modern classic: Chelsea Market in the Meatpacking District, Time Out Market in DUMBO, Essex Market on the Lower East Side, and the various urbanspace-style halls in Midtown all have restrooms meant for a browsing crowd. Buy a coffee if your conscience demands it; the halls are built for wanderers.

Hotels and Museums: The Etiquette Play

Hotel lobby restrooms are a time-honored New York move, and the technique matters more than the hotel. Walk in like you're a guest — purposeful, unhurried, phone maybe in hand — and head past the front desk toward the lobby bar or elevators; restrooms are usually near one or the other. Larger and busier hotels are easier (more foot traffic, less scrutiny). Nobody at a 600-room Midtown hotel is auditing bathroom users. Just don't make it weird, and maybe don't do it four times in one afternoon at the same Ritz.

Museums increasingly have free-to-enter lobby areas with restrooms before the ticket line: the Metropolitan Museum of Art's Great Hall, the Museum of Natural History's entrances, the Brooklyn Museum's lobby. Policies shift, but a polite "is there a restroom before the galleries?" at the admissions desk works more often than not. Cultural institutions generally err on the side of humanity.

Borough-by-Borough Quick Hits

Midtown Manhattan: Bryant Park, Grand Central, Moynihan, Macy's, the public atriums (550 Madison's garden and other privately owned public spaces are required to provide restrooms — look for the POPS signage).

Downtown Manhattan: the Oculus, Fulton Center, Battery Park's comfort stations, the Staten Island Ferry terminal (free ferry, free restrooms, free skyline).

Village/Chelsea: Washington Square Park's comfort station, Chelsea Market, the many NYPL branches (Jefferson Market's gothic tower branch is a landmark in its own right).

Uptown: Central Park's stations, the Met's lobby, Whole Foods Columbus Circle, the Shops at Columbus Circle mall.

Brooklyn: Prospect Park has comfort stations (Picnic House and LeFrak Center areas among them); Brooklyn Bridge Park has restrooms at several piers; Time Out Market and Empire Stores cover DUMBO; Brooklyn Public Library's grand Central branch at Grand Army Plaza covers Park Slope adjacency. Industry City in Sunset Park is a food-hall-sized safety net.

Queens: Flushing Meadows Corona Park has multiple comfort stations; the Queens Center mall and the big food courts of downtown Flushing fill the retail role.

The Bronx and Staten Island: parks and libraries again — plus the Bronx Zoo and New York Botanical Garden if you're ticketed, and the St. George Ferry Terminal on Staten Island.

Why Is It Like This? (And Is It Getting Better?)

Honestly: New York dismantled much of its public restroom infrastructure decades ago. Subway restrooms closed by the hundreds over the late twentieth century amid maintenance and safety concerns, pay toilets were outlawed, and a much-hyped automated street toilet program stalled after installing only a handful of units. What remained was a patchwork of park comfort stations and the informal private-sector system this article describes.

There is movement. The city has committed to building and renovating dozens of public restrooms over the coming years under an initiative announced in 2024 — unofficially and delightfully dubbed "Ur In Luck" — and published an official Google Maps layer of existing public restrooms. Advocates keep pushing for more, because a city that invites the world to visit should probably let the world use a bathroom. Progress is real but slow; in the meantime, knowledge is your utility.

Late Night and Emergencies

After 10 or 11 p.m., the map shrinks dramatically. Parks close, libraries close, department stores close. Your late-night options: 24-hour or late-night diners (a shrinking but surviving breed — buy the coffee, use the restroom, tip well), some fast-food locations with codes on the receipt, hotel lobbies (which never close, and are honestly at their most anonymous at 1 a.m.), Port Authority and Penn/Moynihan during their long operating hours, and the Staten Island Ferry terminals, which run all night because the ferry does. Bars will usually let you in if you're polite and it's not a velvet-rope situation. The 24-hour city is a little less 24-hour than its reputation, but it hasn't abandoned you entirely.

Accessibility and Family Notes

Newer facilities — Moynihan, the Oculus, Bryant Park, renovated Parks Department stations, and any recently built library — have accessible stalls, and the big department stores and museums are generally reliable for wheelchair access and changing tables. Older park comfort stations are hit-or-miss on both counts. If you're pushing a stroller, food halls and department stores are your friends: elevators, wide aisles, family restrooms at the newer ones. NYC law requires changing tables in restrooms of newly built or renovated public buildings regardless of gender, so the situation is genuinely improving for parents. For accessibility specifics, transit hub and museum websites list their facilities, and a restroom-finder app that flags accessible locations saves a lot of scouting.

FAQ

Are there public bathrooms in the NYC subway?

Technically yes, practically mostly no. Only a small fraction of the system's 400-plus stations have open restrooms, and hours are limited. Plan around the big terminals — Grand Central, Penn/Moynihan, Fulton Center — rather than counting on a random station.

Can I use a hotel bathroom if I'm not a guest?

There's no law against walking into a lobby, and larger hotels effectively expect it. Walk in confidently, head toward the lobby bar or elevators, and be unremarkable. If staff ask, a polite "just meeting someone" or an honest "may I use the restroom?" almost always works. Small boutique hotels with a desk three feet from the door are the harder targets.

Do NYC restaurants have to let non-customers use the bathroom?

No. Restrooms in restaurants are for customers, and businesses can enforce that. In practice, a bought coffee converts you into a customer for about two dollars, which is the cheapest bathroom key in Manhattan.

What's the nicest free public restroom in New York?

Bryant Park's, by broad consensus — attended, clean, flowers, classical music. Moynihan Train Hall and the newer NYPL branches are strong runners-up. Set your expectations accordingly everywhere else.

Where can I find a bathroom in NYC late at night?

Late-night diners, hotel lobbies, Port Authority, the Staten Island Ferry terminals, and fast-food spots that stay open late. Parks and libraries are daytime-only. If it's 2 a.m. in Midtown, a big hotel lobby is your most dignified option.
